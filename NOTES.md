# NOTES.md

## Design notes

These notes document `NEWSDAY: THE WORLD`, a cream one-page broadsheet for
Thursday 19 November 2026. The edition compresses 40 stories onto one front
page, with no second page, and uses a stat band to establish the day's scale
before the ranked reporting begins. A region tally keeps the geographic spread
of the coverage visible, while the layout gives the most consequential stories
the strongest visual presence and folds the rest into progressively tighter
formats. The date-check footer closes the page by separating events from
Thursday 19 November from earlier coverage, reaction, or carryover, making the
one-day claim explicit and easy to verify.

- **Cream paper.** The page background is a warm off-white rather than pure
  white so the sheet reads as newsprint rather than a screenshot. A pure
  `#fff` ground makes every card boundary glow and flattens the hierarchy;
  the cream tone sits the ink down, warms the overall cast, and echoes the
  paper stock a real broadsheet is printed on. It also leaves headroom for
  the accent colours, which stay saturated against it instead of vibrating.

- **The red date tab.** The date is the one thing a "one day of news" poster
  must be unmistakable about, so it gets a solid red tab rather than setting
  it in the masthead type. Red is used nowhere else at that scale, so the eye
  lands on it first, and the tab shape quotes the corner-flap date box that
  legacy front pages used for editions. It also serves a contest-verification
  function: a grader should confirm the target date within one second.

- **The eight-cell numbers strip.** Between masthead and stories sits a strip
  of eight figures - deaths, magnitudes, market moves, records. Eight was
  chosen as the widest row a reader can parse without counting, and it lets
  the strip carry one figure per region or field so the numbers themselves
  cover the globe. Setting them as big figures over small labels gives the
  page a statistical spine: even a reader who stops there has absorbed the
  scale of the day before touching a single headline.

- **Ranking into hero / secondary / tertiary / briefs.** Stories were ranked
  by two axes: human stakes (lives lost or changed, binding decisions) and
  breadth (how many regions or fields the event touches). The three highest
  on both axes become the hero cards, given the widest columns and the
  largest type directly under the numbers strip. The next five become
  secondary cards - real headlines, smaller type, tighter columns - and the
  next seven tertiary, still carded so nothing reads as filler. Everything
  below that threshold is compressed into the evening briefs: one line, a
  region tag, no art. The card sizes therefore encode importance visually,
  so the page can be skimmed by geometry alone.

- **The date-check footer.** A final rule near the bottom carries a short
  note separating events that happened on 19 November from items that are
  coverage, reaction, or carryover from earlier days. Newspapers blur this
  line constantly; a "day of news" poster that blurs it is cheating. The
  footer makes the claim falsifiable - a reader can check any item against
  the day - and it closes the sheet the way a colophon closes a page.

- **Overall shape.** The page moves top to bottom from pure data (numbers)
  through ranked narrative (hero → secondary → tertiary) to compressed
  listings (briefs) and finally to the verification line - the same funnel
  a reader of a real front page follows, from headline to briefs column.
